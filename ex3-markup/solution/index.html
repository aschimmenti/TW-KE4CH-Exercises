George Eliot: A Life in Letters and Imagination

By the Editors · 12 May 2024

Early Life and Education

George Eliot was the pen name adopted by Mary Ann Evans, born on 22 November 1819 in Nuneaton, Warwickshire. She was the youngest of five children of Robert Evans, an estate manager, and Christiana Pearson. Her childhood in the rural English Midlands would leave a permanent imprint on her fiction, furnishing it with the landscapes, dialects, and social textures that appear most fully in The Mill on the Floss and Silas Marner.

Evans received a rigorous education unusual for women of her era, attending boarding schools where she developed a command of French, German, Italian, Latin, and Greek. Her early reading encompassed classical literature, theology, and the natural sciences, producing an intellect that contemporaries found formidable.

London and Intellectual Formation

After her father's death in 1849, Evans travelled to Geneva before settling in London in 1851. She joined the editorial circle of the Westminster Review, eventually serving as its effective editor — a position she held without credit, as the formal editorship remained with John Chapman. Through this work she formed lasting friendships with Herbert Spencer and, most consequentially, with George Henry Lewes, the philosopher and literary critic who would become her partner for the next twenty-four years.

Because Lewes was unable to divorce his estranged wife under the laws then in force in England, Evans and Lewes lived together outside of legal marriage, a fact that led to her social ostracism by many of her acquaintances. The relationship was, by all surviving accounts, a source of intellectual sustenance and emotional stability for both.

The Novels

Evans published her first fiction under the masculine pseudonym George Eliot, partly to be taken seriously as a novelist and partly to shield her private life from scrutiny. Her major novels are:

  • Adam Bede (1859)
  • The Mill on the Floss (1860)
  • Silas Marner (1861)
  • Middlemarch (1871–72)
  • Daniel Deronda (1876)

Middlemarch is frequently cited by literary scholars as among the most achieved novels in the English language. Henry James described it as a work that sets a standard for the form, and the novel's intricate management of multiple plots, its sustained psychological analysis, and its engagement with the question of women's vocation in a society that offered them few outlets continue to attract critical attention.

Later Years

Lewes died in November 1878. In May 1880, Evans married John Walter Cross, a banker twenty years her junior who had managed her financial affairs. She died on 22 December 1880 in London, only seven months after the marriage, and was buried in Highgate Cemetery.

Her reputation, which had dimmed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, was substantially restored by the critical work of F. R. Leavis, who placed her at the centre of what he termed the great tradition of the English novel, alongside Charles Dickens, Henry James, and Joseph Conrad. Subsequent feminist scholarship has further deepened understanding of her position as a woman writing in conditions of systematic exclusion.
